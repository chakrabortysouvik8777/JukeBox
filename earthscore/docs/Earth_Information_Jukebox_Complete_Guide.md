# The Earth Information Jukebox — A Beginner’s Guide
## Understanding the problem, its purpose, and what we need to build

Prepared: 16 October 2026  
Audience: beginners in Earth science, sonification, and audio programming.

**The idea:** Create an application that lets someone explore NASA Earth visualizations while hearing meaningful sounds generated from the information being displayed.

**Source status:** The official challenge summary was read directly earlier in this conversation. Its page returned a retrieval error during this update, so this guide does not claim to verify any newly added detailed rules. NASA’s EIC visualization gallery and Web Audio documentation were checked for this guide. The proposed architecture, formulas, features, and implementation steps below are our design suggestions—not additional NASA requirements.

---

## 1. What is the problem statement?

### 1.1 First understand the three important terms

**Earth Information Center (EIC):** NASA’s initiative for communicating information about our changing planet. Its Scientific Visualization Studio gallery includes imagery and animations used in the EIC, covering subjects such as fires, greenhouse gases, and ocean circulation. [2]

**Visualization:** A picture, map, graph, or animation that represents information. For example, a map might use a colour scale to show how a quantity varies across a region.

**Sonification:** Representing information through sound. We define a repeatable rule that connects a property of the information to a property of sound. NASA has examples of this approach for astronomical imagery; those examples are useful background, although this challenge concerns Earth. [3]

A thermometer represents temperature with a number. A colour map represents it with colour. A sonification could represent it with pitch. These are different representations of the same quantity—provided that the mapping is based on valid data.

### 1.2 What NASA is asking us to do

The previously verified official summary asks for an interface, script, or application that combines EIC visual frames with sonifications generated dynamically in real time, making Earth science more accessible and engaging. [1]

A **frame** means one image in a visualization or animation.

In practical terms, our system needs to:

1. Obtain suitable EIC visual material.
2. Identify what information or visual feature we will represent.
3. Define how that information controls sound.
4. Generate the sound while the visualization is displayed or explored.
5. Explain the meaning of the sound to the user.

The problem is therefore a combination of **science communication, accessible interface design, and audio programming**.

### 1.3 An example from start to finish

Suppose we have a visualization with properly documented temperature values for selected places and dates.

A user selects one location and presses Play. As the date changes:

- The map and displayed date update.
- The app reads the temperature corresponding to that location and date.
- A lower value produces a lower pitch.
- A higher value produces a higher pitch.
- A text label shows the actual temperature and unit.

The user can follow the changes visually, listen to them, or use both.

**This is an illustrative design. We have not yet selected or downloaded a temperature dataset matched to an EIC visualization.**

### 1.4 What does “real time” mean here?

We should distinguish two ideas:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Real-time sound generation | The application calculates and produces sound during playback or interaction. |
| Live data | New observations are arriving from an external source. |

A stored visualization from an earlier year can still drive real-time sound. Moving a slider can immediately change the tone generated by the application.

The verified summary requires dynamic sonification; it does not establish that live satellite streaming is compulsory. A stored, clearly dated example is a sensible starting point.

### 1.5 What should the finished prototype demonstrate?

For our proposed minimum version, a visitor should be able to:

1. Select one prepared EIC visualization.
2. Read or hear a short explanation.
3. Start and stop the experience.
4. Explore an image or move through animation time.
5. Hear a consistent response to the information.
6. Consult a legend explaining the sound mapping.

The sound is an intentionally created representation. It is not a recording of the actual noise made by temperature, pollution, or a satellite.

## 2. Why did NASA give this problem statement?

### 2.1 The stated motivation

The official summary identifies a limitation of communicating Earth science through visualizations alone: that approach restricts who can access the information. It asks participants to explore a multisensory way of presenting it. [1]

That is the main stated reason. The following points explain the practical implications rather than claiming additional organizer intentions.

### 2.2 Visual information is not equally accessible to everyone

Consider a map containing thousands of coloured pixels. To understand it, a person may need to see small details, distinguish colours, read the legend, and track changes.

Someone with limited vision may have difficulty with that process. A blind user may need a nonvisual route to the information altogether.

Sound can provide another route, but **adding sound alone does not make an app accessible**. Users must also be able to operate its controls and understand what its sounds mean.

### 2.3 Scientific information needs interpretation

Even a person who can see a visualization clearly may not understand it.

They may ask:

- What does this colour represent?
- Is the picture showing a measurement or a model?
- What period does the animation cover?
- Which changes are meaningful?

A useful jukebox explains the information while allowing exploration. It should make a small scientific idea understandable instead of overwhelming the user with many simultaneous sounds.

### 2.4 The challenge creates room for different skills

Our design can combine:

| Skill | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Earth science | Explain what the selected information means. |
| Programming | Connect visualization, interaction, and audio. |
| Sound design | Create distinct, comfortable, informative sounds. |
| Interface design | Make exploration understandable and usable. |
| Accessibility testing | Check whether people can operate and interpret the system. |

An advanced AI model is not a prerequisite for this combination.

## 3. Why is this solution needed?

### 3.1 To provide another way to access information

The aim is to give users more than one channel for understanding a visualization. Someone might follow a visible pattern, listen to its corresponding sound, or combine both.

For a blind user, the complete experience also needs spoken or screen-reader-accessible descriptions, labelled controls, and keyboard navigation.

For a deaf or hard-of-hearing user, retain the visual display and text values. A multisensory design should not make audio the only route to important information.

### 3.2 To help people explore patterns

Imagine a sequence of values increasing, falling, and increasing again.

A carefully designed pitch mapping might let a listener notice this pattern as a rising, falling, and rising tone. Whether this improves understanding depends on the task, mapping, and user; we should test it rather than assume it.

A simple evaluation question is:

> After learning the sound legend, can the listener identify which of two segments contains the larger value?

### 3.3 To support education and public engagement

Possible settings include a classroom, museum exhibit, science outreach event, or an accessible learning website.

A teacher could show a visualization, play its sonification, and ask students to describe the changes. The app can display the underlying values afterwards so they can check their interpretation.

These are proposed use cases, not promises that every user will learn better with audio.

### 3.4 To turn passive viewing into active exploration

A useful interface gives the user control over a selected region, playback time, or the sound mapping.

This lets someone ask a small question—such as “How does this part differ from that part?”—and explore it themselves.

### 3.5 What the prototype should not claim

This project does not automatically become a disaster forecasting system, an environmental alarm, or a scientific discovery engine.

Representing existing information through sound is the core task. Forecasts and warnings would require additional models, validation, and appropriately chosen data.

## 4. What do we need to solve it?

### 4.1 A clearly defined first use case

Before choosing software, decide:

- Who is the first user?
- What single question should the experience help them answer?
- Which EIC visual asset will we use?
- What exactly will each sound represent?
- How will we check that the representation is correct?

**Proposed first goal:** Let a user explore one EIC image or short visualization and identify meaningful differences using a simple, explained audio mapping.

Begin with one visual asset and one or two sound controls. A large catalogue can come later.

### 4.2 Visual material, scientific data, and metadata

These are related but different:

| Item | What it contains | Why we need it |
|---|---|---|
| Visual asset | An image, animation, or video | What the user sees and explores. |
| Scientific data, when used | Numerical observations or model output | Values that can drive a physically meaningful sonification. |
| Metadata | Units, dates, source, legend, scale, missing-value meaning | Information needed to interpret the visual or numbers correctly. |

The NASA EIC gallery is a verified starting point for visual material. Its examples include ocean circulation, carbon dioxide, forest height, lightning, and night lights. It also links to individual asset pages and daily visualization collections. A downloadable video is not necessarily accompanied by an easy-to-use numerical file. [2]

For each candidate asset, record:

| Question | Example of the information to collect |
|---|---|
| What is represented? | A measured variable, model result, or rendered visual feature. |
| What is the unit? | Degrees, metres, concentration, or no physical unit for a pixel feature. |
| What time does it represent? | Observation date, model period, or historical interval. |
| How is colour assigned? | Continuous scale, categories, or artistic styling. |
| Are values missing? | Masked areas, gaps, clouds, or areas outside coverage. |
| What may we reuse? | Asset-specific credits and reuse conditions. |
| Does the camera move? | A moving viewpoint can change pixels without a physical change. |

Some EIC productions include third-party material with separate reuse restrictions. Check the selected asset’s notes before extracting or remixing it. [2]

### 4.3 Two possible technical approaches

#### Approach A: Generate sound from image features

We examine properties such as brightness, colour categories, or the amount of a selected feature within an image region.

For example, a keyboard-controlled cursor moves across the image. The brightness of a small patch controls pitch.

**Advantages:** A small prototype can work without downloading a large scientific dataset.

**Limitations:** We must describe the sound as representing brightness or another visual feature. Brightness does not automatically equal temperature, pollution, or physical intensity.

Labels, borders, background colours, clouds, and camera motion can all affect the pixels. We need to exclude irrelevant areas.

#### Approach B: Generate sound from numerical scientific values

We obtain numerical data and connect them to the visualization by location, time, and variable.

For example, the sound at a selected location represents a documented value from the matching dataset.

**Advantages:** We can show units, preserve numerical meaning, and compare values more defensibly.

**Limitations:** Data preparation is harder. The dataset and rendered visualization may use different resolutions, dates, projections, or transformations.

**Practical choice:** First inspect the available asset and documentation. Use numerical values when they can be matched reliably. Otherwise build an honest image-feature sonification and clearly label what it represents.

Do not extract arbitrary colours and present the result as a precise physical measurement.

### 4.4 Data formats we may encounter

| Format | Beginner explanation | Potential use |
|---|---|---|
| PNG/JPEG | Still image | Display and image-feature exploration. |
| MP4/WebM | Video | Animated visualization. |
| CSV | Table of rows and columns | Small numerical series. |
| JSON | Structured information readable by an app | Prepared values, metadata, and mappings. |
| NetCDF/HDF | Scientific files with variables and dimensions | Numerical Earth science datasets. |
| GeoTIFF | Image-like grid with geographic information | Geospatial raster data. |

We do not need all these formats. A first version may use one image plus a small metadata JSON file.

### 4.5 The theory we need

#### A. Basic understanding of the selected science

Learn the meaning of the chosen variable and its limitations.

For example, a concentration, a temperature, and a count are different kinds of quantities. A model output is not the same as a direct observation. A change in camera position is not a change in the Earth system.

We only need enough domain understanding to explain the selected example accurately.

#### B. Basic sound concepts

| Concept | Plain-language meaning | Possible use |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Oscillations per second, measured in hertz (Hz) | Controls perceived pitch. |
| Pitch | How high or low a sound seems | Represent increasing or decreasing values. |
| Amplitude | Strength of the audio signal | Influences loudness; use a comfortable range. |
| Rhythm | Timing and repetition of sounds | Represent event frequency or categories. |
| Timbre | Sound character, such as a pure tone or a richer tone | Distinguish different variables or classes. |
| Stereo position | Placement toward left or right | Represent a horizontal position. |

These are possible encodings. We choose and explain them; they are not naturally fixed meanings.

#### C. Normalization: converting values into a usable range

Suppose the chosen values range from a fixed minimum to a fixed maximum.

We convert each value into a number between 0 and 1:

**u = (x − x_min) / (x_max − x_min)**

Here:

- x is the current value.
- x_min is the lower reference value.
- x_max is the upper reference value.
- u is the normalized value.

Use the same reference range for items that users will compare. Rescaling each frame independently could make very different values sound identical.

If x_min equals x_max, use a defined constant-value behaviour instead of dividing by zero. Handle missing values separately; missing information is not a zero measurement.

#### D. Mapping normalized values to pitch

One simple illustrative rule is:

**f = 220 + 660 × u**

Here f is the generated frequency in Hz. This example maps u = 0 to 220 Hz and u = 1 to 880 Hz.

The following temperatures are invented solely to explain the arithmetic:

| Example temperature x | Normalized u, using 10–30 °C | Frequency f |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 °C | 0 | 220 Hz |
| 15 °C | 0.25 | 385 Hz |
| 20 °C | 0.50 | 550 Hz |
| 25 °C | 0.75 | 715 Hz |
| 30 °C | 1 | 880 Hz |

For 20 °C:

1. Subtract the minimum: 20 − 10 = 10.
2. Calculate the reference span: 30 − 10 = 20.
3. Normalize: u = 10 / 20 = 0.5.
4. Calculate frequency: f = 220 + 660 × 0.5 = 550 Hz.

This is a convenient demonstration mapping, not a NASA-prescribed scale. Equal frequency differences do not correspond to equal perceived pitch intervals. A later version could use a logarithmic or musical-note mapping after user testing.

#### E. Sound synthesis and smooth transitions

A basic tone can be represented mathematically as:

**s(t) = A × sin(2πft)**

A controls amplitude, f controls frequency, and t is time in seconds.

A browser audio engine can generate this signal for us. We need to control it, not write a complete audio engine.

Smoothly change pitch and amplitude rather than abruptly restarting tones at every update. This reduces distracting clicks and makes changes easier to follow.

#### F. Synchronization

Three different clocks can exist:

1. The scientific date represented by the data.
2. The playback position in a video or animation.
3. The audio engine’s scheduling clock.

Our application must connect them intentionally.

When the user pauses, seeks, or changes playback speed, audio must follow the currently displayed information. A long-term scientific series may be played in seconds; label this time compression.

### 4.6 Technical tools

The following is a proposed lightweight architecture, not an official mandatory stack:

| Component | Suggested option | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Interface | HTML, CSS, JavaScript; optionally React | Display content and controls. |
| Image/video display | Browser image/video elements and Canvas when needed | Render or inspect visual content. |
| Audio generation | Web Audio API | Generate and control tones in the browser. |
| Small prepared datasets | JSON or CSV | Supply values and metadata. |
| Optional preparation scripts | Python | Clean data and produce small files for the app. |
| Optional backend | FastAPI | Useful only if remote processing or data retrieval requires it. |
| Optional mapping | A suitable map library | Needed only for geographic interaction. |

The Web Audio API provides audio sources, processing nodes, and spatial effects in web applications. It supports oscillators and gain control, which are sufficient building blocks for a basic tone-based prototype. [4]

**We do not need a backend for every version.** A small prepared asset and its data can run entirely in the browser.

For implementation, also check browser behaviour: audio commonly needs a user action such as pressing Play; remote images may have cross-origin restrictions that prevent pixel access. Prefer appropriately permitted local assets for the first demonstration and test the target browser.

### 4.7 Do we need AI or machine learning?

**Not for the basic solution described here.** A rule such as “larger value produces a higher pitch” is an algorithm; it does not require training a model.

Optional later uses include generating plain-language descriptions or classifying visual patterns. These introduce extra validation needs.

A generated narration must not invent measurements or scientific explanations. Add AI only if it solves a specific user problem after the core visualization-and-sound experience works.

### 4.8 Do we need GIS or remote-sensing expertise?

For image-feature sonification, only limited image-processing knowledge may be necessary.

For geographic exploration using numerical data, learn:

- Latitude and longitude.
- How a grid cell corresponds to a place.
- The difference between image coordinates and geographic coordinates.
- Spatial resolution and projection.
- Missing-data masks.
- Matching time and location across sources.

Advanced satellite-image processing is unnecessary if the chosen asset and prepared data already suit the prototype.

### 4.9 Hardware and accounts

A basic prototype needs:

- A laptop or desktop with a modern browser.
- Headphones or speakers.
- Internet access to obtain assets and documentation.
- A code editor.

A dedicated GPU, microphone, physical sensor, or satellite receiver is not necessary for this proposed scope.

Some underlying scientific datasets may require an Earthdata account or another access method. Verify this for the chosen dataset; do not assume every NASA source has the same login or API requirements.

### 4.10 Accessibility features are part of the solution

Include:

- Keyboard-operable Play, Pause, and navigation controls.
- Descriptive labels available to screen readers.
- Visible keyboard focus.
- A simple sound legend and practice example.
- Text equivalents for the values and selected position.
- Volume control and immediate mute.
- Adjustable playback speed.
- A clear indication of missing data.
- An alternative to mouse-only exploration.

Use short, manageable listening segments. Ask intended users to try the controls and interpret the sounds. Do not claim that the prototype is fully accessible solely because it produces audio.

## 5. A practical first prototype

### 5.1 Proposed scope

Build one page with:

1. One suitable EIC visual asset and its source credit.
2. A short explanation of what it represents.
3. A keyboard-accessible way to explore an image region or playback time.
4. One meaningful pitch mapping.
5. A legend, a current-feature/value display, and mute.
6. A clear label stating whether sound represents image features or numerical science data.

Start with one variable or visual feature. Avoid simultaneous layers until users can interpret the first one.

### 5.2 How the components connect

| Stage | Input | Operation | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selection | User chooses an asset | Load visual and metadata | Ready-to-explore display. |
| Exploration | Selected position or time | Locate the relevant information | Feature or scientific value. |
| Preparation | Value and valid reference range | Normalize and check missing data | Mapping input. |
| Sonification | Mapping input | Set pitch and other chosen parameters | Sound generated during interaction. |
| Explanation | Value, units, and mapping | Update labels and accessible text | User can interpret the result. |

### 5.3 Order of implementation

1. **Select and inspect the asset.** Confirm source, meaning, usability, and reuse conditions.
2. **Write the mapping in words.** For example, “pitch represents patch brightness,” with no unsupported physical claims.
3. **Display the asset.** Check that it loads reliably.
4. **Produce a controllable tone.** Add Play, Pause, volume, and mute.
5. **Connect one interaction to one sound parameter.**
6. **Add labels, keyboard controls, and the sound legend.**
7. **Test correctness and understanding.**
8. **Add a second feature only if the first is useful and stable.**

### 5.4 What to test

| Test | What should happen |
|---|---|
| Same input repeated | Same mapped sound. |
| Larger input under an increasing-pitch mapping | Higher pitch. |
| Pause | Playback-linked sound stops or pauses consistently. |
| Seek to another time | Display and sound refer to the same new position. |
| Missing value | Explicit missing-data behaviour, not a fabricated measurement. |
| Keyboard-only interaction | Every essential control remains usable. |
| User comprehension | Listener can explain the mapping after the tutorial. |
| Scientific traceability | Every displayed scientific value has a known source and unit. |

A demonstration can ask the user to compare two segments, identify the larger value or stronger visual feature, and then reveal the corresponding information.

### 5.5 What success means

A successful first version lets someone explain:

- What information they explored.
- What the sound represented.
- How an interaction changed the sound.
- What they learned or compared.
- Which limitations remain.

An attractive interface helps, but correctness, interpretability, and usable controls are what make the experience meaningful.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is the problem statement? | Build an experience that pairs EIC visuals with dynamically generated, meaningful sound. |
| Why was it given? | The official summary highlights the access limitations of visual-only Earth science communication. |
| Why is it needed? | It can provide an additional route to exploration and learning, particularly when paired with genuinely usable accessibility features. |
